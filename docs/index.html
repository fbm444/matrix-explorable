What do matrices mean to you?

Why do we need a way to represent an array of rows and columns of numbers, and to execute computations and operations between them?

[12−20][−12]=[32]\left[\begin{array}{r} 1 & 2 \\ -2 & 0 \end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}{r} -1 \\ 2 \end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}{l} 3 \\ 2 \end{array}\right][1−2​20​][−12​]=[32​]

In school, you may recall reluctantly performing drill after drills of matrix-vector multiplications mechanically. You may even have been taught to memorize several inane formulas.

[abcd][xy]=x[ac]+y[bd]=[ax+bycx+dy] \begin{aligned} {\left[\begin{array}{ll} a & b \\ c & d \end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}{l} x \\ y \end{array}\right] } & =x\left[\begin{array}{l} a \\ c \end{array}\right]+y\left[\begin{array}{l} b \\ d \end{array}\right] \\ & =\left[\begin{array}{l} a x+b y \\ c x+d y \end{array}\right] \end{aligned} [ac​bd​][xy​]​=x[ac​]+y[bd​]=[ax+bycx+dy​]​

Let's explore a deeper, and a more visual intuition behind what matrices represent.

A vector multiplied by a matrix returns yet another vector — it transforms a vector into a new vector. Let's visualize this transformation with an example.

Let's first think about what the coordinates of a vector represent.

In the xyxyxy-coordinate plane, any vector can be thought of as the sum of two scaled vectors: the unit vector in the xxx-direction, and the unit vector in the yyy-direction.

[−12]=−1[10]+2[01]\textcolor{#04cefb}{\left[\begin{array}{r} \textcolor{#ff79c6}{-1} \\ \textcolor{#bd93f9}{2} \end{array}\right]}=\textcolor{#ff79c6}{-1}\left[\begin{array}{l} \textcolor{#ff57b6}{1} \\ \textcolor{#ff57b6}{0} \end{array}\right] +\textcolor{#bd93f9}{2}\left[\begin{array}{l} \textcolor{#aa73f7}{0} \\ \textcolor{#aa73f7}{1} \end{array}\right] [−12​]=−1[10​]+2[01​] These are also known as our standard basis vectors. The vector's coordinates encode the amount to scale each individual basis vector, before adding them up.

This scaling and addition of vectors is called a linear combination, and every vector can be expressed as a linear combination of basis vectors.

[abcd][xy]=x[ac]+y[bd]=[ax+bycx+dy] \begin{aligned} {\left[\begin{array}{ll} \textcolor{#ff57b6}{a} & \textcolor{#aa73f7}{b} \\ \textcolor{#ff57b6}{c} & \textcolor{#aa73f7}{d} \end{array}\right]\textcolor{#04cefb}{\left[\begin{array}{l} \textcolor{#ff79c6}{x} \\ \textcolor{#bd93f9}{y} \end{array}\right]} } & =\textcolor{#ff79c6}{x}\left[\begin{array}{l} \textcolor{#ff57b6}{a} \\ \textcolor{#ff57b6}{c} \end{array}\right]+\textcolor{#bd93f9}{y}\left[\begin{array}{l} \textcolor{#aa73f7}{b} \\ \textcolor{#aa73f7}{d} \end{array}\right] \\ & =\textcolor{#04cefb}{\left[\begin{array}{l} \textcolor{#ff57b6}{a} \textcolor{#ff79c6}{x}+\textcolor{#aa73f7}{b} \textcolor{#bd93f9}{y} \\ \textcolor{#ff57b6}{c} \textcolor{#ff79c6}{x}+\textcolor{#aa73f7}{d} \textcolor{#bd93f9}{y} \end{array}\right]} \end{aligned} [ac​bd​][xy​]​=x[ac​]+y[bd​]=[ax+bycx+dy​]​

Did you notice anything similar with the expression for matrix-vector multiplication? A vector multiplied with a matrix can also be expressed as a linear combination; only this time the standard basis vectors are replaced by the columns of the matrix.

[12−20][−12]=−1[1−2]+2[20]=[32]\begin{aligned} {\left[\begin{array}{r} \textcolor{#ff57b6}{1} & \textcolor{#aa73f7}{2} \\ \textcolor{#ff57b6}{-2} & \textcolor{#aa73f7}{0} \end{array}\right] \textcolor{#04cefb}{\left[\begin{array}{r} \textcolor{#ff79c6}{-1} \\ \textcolor{#bd93f9}{2} \end{array}\right]} } & =\textcolor{#ff79c6}{-1}\left[\begin{array}{r} \textcolor{#ff57b6}{1} \\ \textcolor{#ff57b6}{-2} \end{array}\right]+\textcolor{#bd93f9}{2} \left[\begin{array}{r} \textcolor{#aa73f7}{2} \\ \textcolor{#aa73f7}{0} \end{array}\right] \\ & = \left[\begin{array}{l} \textcolor{#04cefb}{3} \\ \textcolor{#04cefb}{2} \end{array}\right] \end{aligned} [1−2​20​][−12​]​=−1[1−2​]+2[20​]=[32​]​

In other words, a matrix can be viewed as a way of packaging information about the new basis vectors that we want. This is the core insight: a matrix transforms a vector by transforming the original basis vectors; creating an entirely new coordinate system.

Again, the transformed vector is a linear combination of the new basis vectors, which are scaled by the coordinates of the original vector.

In that vein, a matrix transformation appears to warp and transform space. To get a visceral feel of this, let's visualize what happens to not just a single vector, but a sample of vectors in space, each multiplied by the same matrix.

In order to make the space less visually cluttered, we can represent each vector with just its tip as a point in space. We'll transform the grid lines along too, overlaying on top a copy of the original.

The transformation appears to rotate and stretch the space, accordingly with where the new basis vectors land.

As we'll see when you have a chance to tinker around with different basis vectors, a matrix performs a particular kind of transformation, called a linear transformation. Visually, you'll notice that:

  • All lines in the original space remain as lines, without getting curved, and
  • Origin remains fixed in place.

As an example, all grid lines stay parallel and evenly spaced after the transformation.

Recap

  • Any vector can be expressed as the addition of scaled basis vectors, i.e. a linear combination of basis vectors.
  • A matrix can be viewed as a way to package information about a linear transformation. The columns of a matrix represent where the new basis vectors land after the transformation.
  • Matrix-vector multiplication is a way to compute where a given vector lands after the transformation defined by a matrix.

New chapter

The Determinant

A matrix tells us where ı^\hat{\imath}^ and ȷ^\hat{\jmath}^​ land. The grid shows how those two moves stretch, tilt, or flip the rest of the plane.

For a 2×22 \times 22×2 matrix, the determinant is a number whose size, ignoring its sign, tells us how much the transformation scales area and whose sign tells us whether it flips the plane over. Every square matrix — one with as many rows as columns — has a determinant, in any number of dimensions. We'll start in two dimensions, where we can see its meaning as area.

A determinant of 222 doubles every area, while 0.50.50.5 halves it. Let's see where that number comes from by following a square of area 111.

What the Determinant Measures

Start with the unchanged grid, with ı^\hat{\imath}^ and ȷ^\hat{\jmath}^​ at their original positions. They form two sides of the unit square: one unit wide and one unit tall.

The identity matrix leaves this square unchanged, so its determinant is +1+1+1. After a transformation, the square's area gives the size of the determinant; we attach a minus sign if the square has flipped over, which is what we mean by signed area. Scroll through the examples: each starts from the unit square, and the top-left readout shows its signed area as it changes.

Stretch: ı^\hat{\imath}^ lands twice as far from the origin, while ȷ^\hat{\jmath}^​ stays in place. The square becomes a 2×12 \times 12×1 rectangle: twice the area, with no flip, so the determinant is +2+2+2.

Compress: ȷ^\hat{\jmath}^​ becomes half as long, while the square's width stays 111. The area is now 0.50.50.5, with no flip, so the determinant is +0.5+0.5+0.5.

Shear: ȷ^\hat{\jmath}^​ leans to the right, turning the square into a parallelogram. Its base and perpendicular height are still 111, so its area and determinant both stay 111 even though its shape changes.

Rotate: both basis vectors turn 90∘90^\circ90∘ counterclockwise around the origin, carrying the square with them. The square keeps its area and does not flip over, so the determinant stays +1+1+1.

Reflect: ȷ^\hat{\jmath}^​ moves below the xxx-axis, flipping the square over. Orientation describes whether the turn from the first basis vector to the second, through the smaller angle, is counterclockwise or clockwise; this reflection reverses it. The final area is still 111, but the determinant is −1-1−1: the minus sign records the flip, not a negative amount of area.

During the flip, the square flattens to a line before opening below the axis. At that instant its area and determinant are both 000. A continuous change from a positive determinant to a negative one must pass through zero.

Here a,b,c,da, b, c, da,b,c,d are the four entries of the matrix, and det⁡\detdet means determinant. This formula computes the same signed area we've been watching. Choose a transformation below to compare its matrix, its final determinant, and the shape it produces.

The Determinant and Invertibility

A transformation is invertible if it can be undone so that every output returns to exactly one original input. A determinant of 000 tells us this is impossible: the plane has collapsed onto a line or a point. Let's follow a few vectors to see what gets lost.

The shaded shape now follows two chosen vectors, so its starting area may differ from 111. The readout labels its signed area separately from the transformation's determinant: the determinant tells us how that starting area is scaled.

Here are two different vectors, (1,1)\textcolor{#04cefb}{(1, 1)}(1,1) and (1,3)\textcolor{#f1fa8c}{(1, 3)}(1,3), which span a parallelogram of area 222. As you scroll, a collapse sends the whole plane onto the xxx-axis, and both vectors land at the same point, (1,0)\textcolor{#ff5555}{(1, 0)}(1,0). The shaded area shrinks to 000, the transformation's determinant is 000, and the faded arrows show the two different starting vectors.

The same happens to these three vectors: their tips all have x=−2x = -2x=−2, but different heights. They all land at (−2,0)\textcolor{#ff5555}{(-2, 0)}(−2,0) because this collapse preserves each vector's x-coordinate and discards its y-coordinate.

Now collapse onto the yyy-axis. These three vectors all have y=2y = 2y=2, so they land together at (0,2)(0, 2)(0,2): this time their x-coordinates are lost. The shaded area and determinant both fall to 000 again.

The line doesn't have to be an axis: here (2,0)\textcolor{#04cefb}{(2, 0)}(2,0) and (0,2)\textcolor{#f1fa8c}{(0, 2)}(0,2) both land at (1,1)\textcolor{#ff5555}{(1, 1)}(1,1) on the diagonal line. A line has no two-dimensional area, so the square they span is flattened from area 444 to 000. The factor that scales a nonzero area to zero is 000 — that is why this transformation's determinant is zero.

Now try to reverse the first collapse, using only its output (1,0)\textcolor{#ff5555}{(1, 0)}(1,0). Did it come from (1,1)\textcolor{#04cefb}{(1, 1)}(1,1) or from (1,3)\textcolor{#f1fa8c}{(1, 3)}(1,3)? Both inputs gave the same output, so the output alone cannot tell us which one to recover.

In fact, every vector whose tip lies on this dashed line lands at (1,0)\textcolor{#ff5555}{(1, 0)}(1,0). No single reverse rule can recover all their original heights from that one output. Scrolling backward can replay the starting points we saved for the animation, but the collapsed output itself does not contain that information.

The horizontal stretch doubles each x-coordinate and keeps each y-coordinate. Its determinant of +2+2+2 scales the shaded area from 222 to 444. To undo it, halve each output's x-coordinate and keep its y-coordinate — every vector returns to its own starting point, so the transformation is invertible.

The reflection across the xxx-axis has determinant −1-1−1: it flips orientation and preserves area. Reflect again across the same axis, and every vector returns to where it started. A negative determinant still allows an inverse; a zero determinant does not.

The determinant's size, ignoring its sign, tells us how much area is scaled, and its sign tells us whether orientation is preserved or reversed. A determinant of 000 means the plane collapses onto a line or a point, losing the information needed to recover every input. A square matrix is invertible exactly when its determinant is nonzero.

Beyond Two Dimensions: Determinant

In three dimensions, add a third basis vector, k^\hat{k}k^, along the z-axis: the three vectors span a unit cube of volume 111. A 3×33 \times 33×3 matrix sends those three vectors to new places, and its determinant gives the cube's signed volume: size measures volume, and a minus sign records a flip.

Area and volume are just the two- and three-dimensional cases of n-dimensional volume. The same idea works for an n×nn \times nn×n matrix in any dimension. An n-dimensional parallelotope is a possibly slanted box built from n independent edge vectors — a parallelogram in two dimensions, a solid like the sheared cube in three, and their counterpart in higher dimensions.

The matrix transforms the unit n-dimensional cube into the parallelotope spanned by its columns. Its n-dimensional volume is the absolute value of the determinant, and that same factor scales the volume of any other parallelotope under the transformation.

In every dimension, a positive determinant preserves orientation and a negative one reverses it. A zero determinant flattens the parallelotope into fewer dimensions, leaving zero n-dimensional volume and losing information. The transformation is invertible exactly when its determinant is nonzero.

Beyond Two-Dimensions

We've seen what a determinant measures in three dimensions. Now let's look at how a 3×33 \times 33×3 matrix moves an individual vector, using the same column-by-column reasoning as before.

To make up three dimensions, we have yet another standard basis vector — the unit vector in the zzz-direction. This also means we're now fiddling around with vectors of length 333 — representing the xyzxyzxyz coordinates — and matrices of size 3×33\times33×3.

The concept of matrix transformations in 3D is exactly the same. The three basis vectors are transformed to their new locations, warping space along with them. These locations are completely determined by the columns of the matrix.

Originally, any vector is composed of a linear combination of these three standard basis vectors. To figure out the where the vector lands after the transformation, it is a linear combination of the transformed basis vectors, each scaled by the respective coordinates in the starting vector.

From these visually-focused examples we've seen thus far, the most obvious application of matrix transformations would be that of computer graphics. In fact, this is precisely how this article was built! Matrices provide a language to rotate, scale and translate vectors and points and consequently entire objects in 2D or 3D space.
